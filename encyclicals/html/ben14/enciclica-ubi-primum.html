ENCYCLICAL UBI PRIMUM OF THE SUPREME PONTIFF BENEDICT XIV To the Venerable Brothers, Patriarchs, Primates, Archbishops, and Bishops. Pope Benedict XIV. Venerable Brothers, greetings and Apostolic Blessings. When it pleased God, rich in mercy, to place Our humble person on the supreme See of Blessed Peter and to entrust to Us—though no merit of Our own merits Us—the vicarious power of Our Lord Jesus Christ for the governance of His entire Church, it seemed to Us that that divine voice resounded in Our ears: “Feed my lambs; feed my sheep”; that is, that it was entrusted to the Roman Pontiff, the successor of Peter himself, the mission of guiding not only the lambs of the Lord’s flock—which are the peoples scattered throughout the earth—but also the sheep, that is, the bishops, who, like mothers to their lambs, bring forth the peoples in Jesus Christ and give birth to them a second time. Accept, therefore, Brothers, along with this letter of ours, the words of your Shepherd as well. Called to the task of encouraging others, in the fullness of the mandate entrusted to us by God, you understand how much it is our heartfelt desire—in our own invitations and exhortations—not to neglect any of our duties, and how great is the strength of our paternal love for you: by virtue of which, we are led to desire above all that eternal joys may flow to the Shepherds from the well-being of the holy flock. 1. First of all, indeed, work diligently and to the best of your ability so that moral integrity and the study of divine worship may shine forth in the clergy, and so that ecclesiastical discipline may be preserved intact and sound, and restored where it has fallen into disrepair. It is well known, in fact, that nothing more effectively instructs, inspires, and enkindles the entire people to piety, religion, and the norms of the Christian life than the example of those who have dedicated themselves to the divine ministry. Therefore, the discernment of your mind must be directed first and foremost toward ensuring that, through careful selection, those are admitted to the clerical ranks from whom one can reasonably expect that their lives will be an object of admiration for all who walk in the law of the Lord, who progress from virtue to virtue and, through their work, bring spiritual benefit to your Churches. Certainly, it is better to have few ministers—but honest, suitable, and useful ones—than many who are in no way destined for the edification of the Body of Christ, which is the Church. You, Brothers, are well aware of the prudence required of bishops in this matter by the Sacred Canons; therefore, do not allow yourselves to be swayed from what is prescribed (which must be observed in full)—neither by any human regard, nor by ill-advised suggestions from your surroundings, nor by the requests of patrons. Above all, we must observe the Apostle’s precept not to ordain anyone too hastily when it comes to promoting someone to the Sacred Orders and the Most Holy Ministries, of which nothing is more divine. In fact, the age required by the sacred laws of the Church for each Order is not sufficient on its own, nor should the path to higher offices be opened indiscriminately—as if by right—to all those who have already been admitted to some lower Order. You must investigate with great care and diligence whether the way of life of those who have assumed the first ministries has been in accordance with the requirements, and whether their progress in the sacred doctrines has been such that they should truly be deemed worthy to hear the words: “Go higher.” Moreover, how much better it is for some to remain at a lower rank than to be promoted to a higher one, which would pose a greater danger to them and be a cause of scandal to others. 2. And since it is of the utmost importance that those called to the service of the Lord be formed from a young age in piety, integrity of character, and canonical discipline (like young saplings in their early stages), you must therefore ensure that, where seminaries for clerics have not yet been established, they be established as soon as possible, or that existing ones be expanded if, given the situation of the Church, there is a need for a greater number of students, using for this purpose the resources that the bishops already have the authority to procure, and to which We will add others if You inform Us of their necessity. Indeed, it is essential that these colleges themselves be subject to your special care: inspecting them frequently; examining the conduct, character, and academic progress of each adolescent; assigning qualified teachers and men endowed with an ecclesiastical spirit to their formation; occasionally honoring their literary exercises or ecclesiastical services with your presence; and finally, granting certain privileges to those who have given the most evident proof of their merits and have earned the greatest praise. You will not regret having provided such nourishment to these young saplings as they grow; indeed, your efforts will bear consoling fruit in the abundant supply of good workers. Undoubtedly, bishops were often in the habit of lamenting that the harvest was plentiful but the laborers few; but perhaps they should also have lamented their own failure to devote the necessary zeal to training laborers equal to and suited for the harvest. In fact, good and capable workers are not born, but are made; and it is above all up to the diligence and commitment of the bishops to ensure that they are made. 3. Furthermore, it is of the utmost importance that the care of souls be entrusted to those who, through their doctrine, piety, purity of conduct, and outstanding examples of good works, can shed light on others to such an extent that they are regarded as the light and salt of the people. These are truly your foremost collaborators in instructing, guiding, purifying, leading along the path to salvation, and inspiring the flock entrusted to you to practice Christian virtues. It is therefore easy to understand how deeply you must care that those who are deservedly judged to be the most suitable for effectively guiding the flock be chosen for parish office. Above all, however, insist that all those who care for souls nourish the people entrusted to them with wholesome words (at least on Sundays and other holy days of obligation), according to their own and the people’s capacity, teaching everything that the faithful of Christ must learn for their salvation and explaining the articles of divine law, the dogmas of the Faith, and instilling in children the rudiments of the Faith itself, after having completely eradicated every bad habit, wherever it may appear. Indeed, how can they listen if there is no preacher? Or how can the people understand a law that prescribes a right belief and right conduct if the shepherds of souls have been lazy, negligent, and inactive in that office? It is impossible to fully comprehend in one’s heart or explain in words how much harm is caused to the Christian Republic by the negligence of those entrusted with the care of souls, especially in teaching the catechism to children. It will also be of great benefit if you ensure that both those who care for souls and those who are called to hear the confessions of penitents devote a few days each year to spiritual exercises: surely, in such a pious retreat, they will be renewed in their spiritual life and, from on high, will be endowed with the virtues necessary to fulfill with greater diligence and zeal those duties that are directed toward the glory of God and the benefit and spiritual well-being of their neighbors. 4. Indeed, you already know, Brothers, that by divine command all shepherds of souls are ordered to know their flock and to nourish them through the preaching of the divine Word, the administration of the sacraments, and the example of every good work; but, as is obvious, those who do not watch over, assist, and assiduously guard the Lord’s vineyard—over which they have been appointed as guardians—cannot at all fulfill these and other pastoral duties. Therefore, you must remain at your post and maintain, within your Church or diocese, the personal residence to which you are bound by the terms of your office, in accordance with what is clearly declared and prescribed by numerous decrees of the Ecumenical Councils and the Constitutions of Our Predecessors. Beware, moreover, of believing that it is permissible for bishops to be absent for three months each year on a whim or for any reason whatsoever. For this to be lawful for bishops, a just cause must necessitate such an absence, and at the same time, it must be ensured that no harm will result to the flock. Remember, moreover, that the future Judge will be the One in whose eyes all things are laid bare; therefore, ensure that your case is truly such that it will find favor with this supreme Prince of Shepherds, who will soon call you to account for the blood of the sheep entrusted to you. In this trial, the Shepherd will try in vain to defend himself with the excuse that the wolf stole and devoured the sheep while he was absent and unaware; for, if the matter is examined thoroughly, it becomes evident that no evil or scandal manifests itself in such a neglected diocese that is not attributable to the one who was supposed to call back, through his admonitions, those subjects who strayed from the right path, to urge them on by example, encourage them with words, and keep them in check with authority and charity. Who, then, does not understand that it is far better to address matters elsewhere, when necessary, through others, rather than by the Bishop himself residing outside his diocese; and that the duty, certainly the most urgent of all—to care for and guide the flock—should be carried out directly by the Bishop and not through intermediaries? For, however suitable and esteemed such ministers may be, the flock is not as accustomed to heeding their voice as it is to the voice of its true shepherd; and it is well known from extensive experience that their vicarious work does not sufficiently replace the vigilance and action of the Bishop himself, who is aided by the special grace of the Holy Spirit. 5. We admonish and exhort you, Brothers, regarding these matters, for just as in every household nothing is more useful than for the head of the household himself to frequently oversee everything carefully and, through his vigilance, to foster the industriousness and diligence of his household members, so we command you to visit your own Churches and dioceses yourselves (unless a serious and legitimate cause arises that requires this task to be entrusted to others), so that you yourselves may know your own sheep and the face of your own flock. That most certain maxim, which we have recalled above—that no excuse is accepted for the shepherd if the wolf devours the sheep and the shepherd is unaware of it—is certainly inspired by great fear and dread. Undoubtedly, the bishop will be unaware of many things; many will remain hidden from him, or at the very least, he will learn of them later than necessary, if he does not visit every part of his diocese. If he does not see, hear, and verify things in person everywhere, he will not know to which evils to apply the remedy, what their causes are, or how he can, with foresight, ensure that once they have been suppressed, they cannot reappear. Moreover, human frailty is such that in the Lord’s field (the care of which is entrusted to the Bishop), weeds, thorns, and useless and harmful plants gradually grow if the cultivator does not return often to cut them down; therefore, the very flourishing achieved through his vigilant efforts will eventually fade with the passage of time. But it is not enough merely for you to visit the dioceses and ensure their proper administration through your appropriate directives: you still have the task of making every effort to verify that everything agreed upon during the visits is truly put into practice. In fact, laws—even the best ones—will be of no use if what was established in words is not correctly translated into action by those entrusted with the task. Therefore, after you have prepared salutary remedies to expel or ward off the maladies of souls, let not your zeal wane; rather, you must urge with all your energy the implementation of the provisions you have issued; and you will achieve this goal above all through repeated visits. 6. Finally, to put it briefly, Brothers, it is fitting that in every sacred and ecclesiastical function and in every exercise of divine worship and piety, you yourselves be promoters, leaders, and teachers, so that both the clergy and the entire flock may draw light as it were from the radiance of your holiness and be warmed by the flame of your charity. Therefore, in the frequent and devout offering of the most holy Sacrifice, during the solemn celebration of Masses, in the administration of the sacraments, in the performance of the Divine Offices, in the splendor and radiance of the churches, in the discipline of your household and your family, in your love for the poor and the aid you provide them, in visiting and assisting the sick, in welcoming pilgrims, and finally, in every manifestation of Christian virtue, you will be the model for your flock, so that all may imitate you, just as you imitate Christ—as befits bishops, whom the Holy Spirit has appointed to govern the Church of God, which He won with His blood. Think often of the Apostles, whose place you have taken, so that you may follow in their footsteps in enduring hardships, vigils, and anxieties; in keeping the wolves away from your flocks, in uprooting the roots of vice, in expounding the Gospel law, and in leading those who have sinned back to wholesome penance. Almighty and merciful God will certainly be at your side; His help makes everything possible for us. Nor will the assistance of the religious princes fail you, as we firmly believe. Furthermore, this Holy See will not fail to provide you with assistance whenever you deem Our apostolic authority necessary. Therefore, with great courage and great confidence, come to Us, all of you whom We love as brothers, collaborators, and Our crown, in the name of Jesus Christ; come to the Holy Roman Church, your mother, guide, and teacher, and that of all the Churches, from which the Religion originated and where lies the rock of the Faith, the source of unity among priests, and the doctrine of incorrupt truth; for nothing, in fact, could be more desired or more pleasing to Us than to serve, together with you, the glory of God and to labor for the preservation and spread of the Catholic Faith; to save souls, We would, if necessary, shed Our very blood and give Our lives with the greatest joy. And now may the great and certain reward that awaits you spur and encourage you in your race. For when the Prince of Shepherds appears, you will receive the incorruptible crown of glory, the crown of righteousness that has been reserved for the faithful interpreters of God’s mysteries and for the steadfast and vigilant guardians of the house of Israel, which is the Holy Church of God Himself. We, who—however unworthy we may be—act as His representatives on earth, most affectionately bless you, Brothers, and with paternal love impart Our Apostolic Blessing also to your clergy and your faithful people. Given in Rome, at Saint Mary Major, on December 3, 1740, the first year of Our Pontificate.
